Key recurring messages from the session

The panel cannot influence individual cases and cannot comment on newly announced government proposals until they have gone through the legislative process. They can take community feedback back to the right Home Office teams and signpost people to help.

Tell the Home Office everything. Caseworkers only know what you send them. Keep your application updated with any change in your circumstances, your studies, work and life in the UK, ideally in a cover letter quoting your Home Office reference.

Every case is considered on its own merits, not by nationality. The pause on Syrian decisions has ended, but the backlog it created, the reviews linked to the change in Syria and the need for legal advice mean decisions are slow.

Use your MP. If a complaint to the Home Office does not resolve a delay, your MP can raise it directly and can refer it to the Parliamentary and Health Service Ombudsman.

If you are refused, appeal on time. The appeal is heard by an independent tribunal, not the Home Office, and it supersedes a reconsideration request. Check the deadline in your refusal letter.

Only use regulated advisers. The panel regularly sees people who paid someone who never submitted an application. Use the official adviser search, and use a Home Office surgery to check whether an application actually exists.

Community concerns raised for the Home Office

These points were raised by attendees as feedback rather than questions with a direct answer. The panel said it would take the community's concerns back to the relevant teams.

  • Very few decisions appear to have been made on Syrian settlement (ILR) applications since the pause was lifted, and attendees asked for published figures on processing times and numbers decided.
  • Applicants report receiving identical standard updates ("exceptionally complex issues", "passed to another government department for further checks") every six months, which feels like a blanket approach despite the assurance that each case is considered individually.
  • Current UK travel advice warns British nationals against travel to Syria for security reasons, while refusals for Syrians rely on Syria being safe to return to. Attendees asked how these two positions are reconciled.
  • As the pause was not the applicants' fault, attendees asked for the backlog to be cleared in chronological order of application, or for an exceptional solution.
  • Long waits are affecting careers and education: for example, an NHS doctor whose access to specialty training is limited without ILR, and young people unable to plan university study or afford international fees.
  • Some older applicants without digital skills or English rely on intermediaries and have little visibility of their applications.
  • Loss of access to eVisa share codes while settlement applications are pending is affecting people's right to work and right to rent checks.
